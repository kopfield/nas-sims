The Physics of Radioactive Decay

Radioactive decay is the process by which an unstable atomic nucleus loses energy by radiation. This is a stochastic (random) process at the level of single atoms, meaning it's impossible to predict exactly when one particular atom will decay.

However, for a large collection of atoms, the rate of decay is predictable and follows a clear pattern. This is described by the concept of half-life (T½), which is the time required for half of the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay.

The number of remaining nuclei, N(t), after a time (t) can be calculated using the formula:
N(t) = N₀ * (0.5)(t / T½)
where N₀ is the initial number of nuclei.

This is equivalent to the exponential decay formula:
N(t) = N₀ * e-λt
where λ (lambda) is the decay constant. The decay constant is related to the half-life by: λ = ln(2) / T½. The "Theoretical Decay" curve on the graph is plotted using this formula.

This simulation models the random decay of each individual nucleus. The "Simulated Decay" line shows the actual count of remaining nuclei, which fluctuates around the smooth theoretical curve, demonstrating the statistical nature of the process.
